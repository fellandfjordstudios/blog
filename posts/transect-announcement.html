Transect is out—and you can use the assets

Transect is out. It’s a first-person walk through a procedural valley that you can play in your browser. Follow the trail, try the weather controls, or stay out until dark and see how the place looks.

I’ll keep working on it. I’ve also put together an asset catalog where other developers can browse, preview, and download assets for their own games.

Both came from wanting to make good graphical assets and actually use them in a game. Looking at a model on its own only told me so much. I wanted to know how it would look across a hillside, with a lot of other things around it, running on my phone.

Why a valley?

A model can look pretty good when you’ve picked the camera angle and lighting for it. I needed to check what happened when I didn’t have that control. Could I still make out its shape from a distance? Did it look odd when I used it twenty times? And could the device handle a whole landscape of these things?

A trail through a valley gave me a useful way to test that. I could walk past the same assets at different distances, change the time of day, and try different weather. Some problems only showed up once the light changed. Others showed up when I added enough assets to slow things down.

As I worked on the landscape, I added touch controls, weather, and sound. Eventually I liked walking around in it enough to want to share it.

Trying it on my phone

The browser was a convenient way to check the work. I could open a link on my phone and try the latest build without installing anything.

The preview tooling had a 64 MB artifact limit. That wasn’t a download target I’d researched; it was just the limit I had to work with. Still, it was useful to have a reason to watch the size. Smaller builds download faster, and I had to think about what each addition would cost.

Generating assets on the device helped keep the download small. Of course, then I had to wait for the phone to make them. I spent enough time on loading that I half wanted to call it Loading Screen: The Game.

The standalone build now uses threads where they’re available. The preview still needs to work without them. Preparing more before you start walking can help with pauses later, but it also means a longer loading screen and more data sitting in memory. It took quite a few experiments to work out which parts were worth doing upfront.

I’ve written about those experiments in Loading a procedural world in Godot on the web, including a cave hitch that threads didn’t fix.

Using the assets

The asset catalog has previews, specifications, and available downloads so you can see whether something will be useful in your own project. Available free builds use CC BY 4.0, with attribution information included in the download. The generators are private; the resulting assets are available under their stated licences.

You’ll still want to try them with your own lighting and on your target hardware. Something that works in my valley might need adjustments in your scene. But you can download what’s available and try it without having to build the tools that made it.

I’ll use this blog to write about new assets, changes to Transect, and the engineering work as I go. For now, you can take a walk and have a look through the catalog.
